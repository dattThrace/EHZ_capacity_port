  2. A chronicle: from worms through billiards to symplectic systoles
2.1. Worms. While in this paper we focus on translation covers, the history of covering
problems starts with congruence covers, also known as displacement covers or universal
covers. More specifically, given a collection of shapes {Tj | j ∈ J} to be covered, one is
asked to find the smallest (in some sense) shape K such that for any j ∈ J there is an
isometry fj of the euclidean plane such that fj (Tj ) ⊂ K. In other words, K contains a
congruent copy of each Tj . Apparently, the first covering problem is due to H. Lebesgue,
who asked in 1914 about the area-minimizing congruence cover for all sets of unit diameter
([10, Chapter 11.14, Problem 1]). The question is still open; see [11, 17] for the current
best bounds.
   If all fj are required to be translations, a shape K for which one can choose translations
fj with fj (Tj ) ⊂ K is called a translation cover for the family {Tj | j ∈ J}. The first
famous question on translation covers was asked by S. Kakeya in 1917: what is the
smallest area of a shape that contains a translated copy of every “needle” (any line
segment of unit length)? The solution among convex shapes was shown by J. Pál to be an
equilateral triangle [31], whereas Besicovitch constructed non-convex covers of arbitrarily
small area [7].
   A famous unsolved problem, known as L. Moser’s worm problem [10, Chapter 11.4,
Problem 4], is to find the area-minimizing congruence cover for the collection of curves of
length 1. In [40], J. Wetzel proved a lower bound for the area of such a congruence cover,
which is still unbeaten. More importantly for us, in the same paper Wetzel proposed a
related problem: find the smallest area of a convex translation cover for all closed curves
of length 1. We call it Wetzel’s worm problem, though given that the curves are closed,
we could have called it a ringworm problem as well. This problem is open as well [10,
Chapter 11.4, Problem 8], with the best lower bound due to Wetzel himself [40]. A
classical observation due to V. Klee [23], following directly from Helly’s theorem, leads to
an equivalent reformulation of Wetzel’s worm problem [9, Remark 1], which we stated in
the introduction: a convex set K is a translation cover for any closed curve of unit length if
and only if it is a translation cover for any triangle of unit perimeter (including degenerate
ones, which are line segments of length 1/2), so one might look for the smallest translation
cover for triangles of unit perimeter. The best (in terms of area) known example of such a
set K (and the best upper bound for the area in Wetzel’s problem) is due to H. Mallée [27],
and we describe this example now. In Figure 1, left, one sees a parabola with a vertical
directrix (not shown) and focus F , so that every ray of light passing through F reflects
off the parabola and exits horizontally. An arc of this parabola can be reflected under the
D3 dihedral group to obtain the shape K, shown in Figure 1, right, circumscribed around
a regular triangle (one of the dashed triangles) having F as one of its vertices and with
its opposite side horizontal. Inside K one sees three dashed closed curves—two triangles
and one line segment traversed twice—of equal length.

lengths are measured and the collection of triangles to be covered depend on a parameter—
a convex polygon Q. Some instances of this problem were given in the introduction.

2.2. Billiards. An important insight of [9] is an equivalent reformulation of Wetzel’s
worm problem in terms of billiards.1 The idea is to consider the triangle (perhaps, de-
generate) of shortest perimeter that cannot be covered by a translate of the interior of
K; it turns out it can be translated so that all its vertices lie on the boundary of K, and
its sides are related to the boundary in a way that is akin to the billiard reflection rule.
The only catch is that the corresponding boundary point does not have to belong to a
smooth part of the boundary of K, so the billiard rule needs to be generalized. We say
that a polygonal curve u → v → w, where u, w ∈ K, v ∈ ∂K, u ̸= v ̸= w, satisfies the
generalized billiard reflection rule at v, if there is a support line ℓ for K at v such that
the outward normal vector ν to ℓ forms equal angles with the vectors v − u and w − v.
The central objects of this subsection are closed, or periodic, generalized billiard orbits.
We say that a billiard orbit is m-periodic if it has m reflection points in ∂K. Now we can
formulate the elegant result of K. Bezdek and R. Connelly [9, Lemma 8], using modern
terminology.

   The next milestone in our story is the observation made in [2]: Theorem 2.1 remains
valid when R2 is endowed with an arbitrary norm, with lengths and billiard reflections
defined accordingly. Given a compact convex set Q ⊂ R2 containing the origin in its inte-
rior, one can define the (asymmetric) norm ∥ · ∥Q as the Minkowski functional associated
to Q◦ , the polar set of Q (see Section 3 for the details). This norm defines a non-euclidean
way of measuring lengths, which we call Q-lengths. Thinking about light propagation in
an anisotropic medium and employing Fermat’s principle, one is led to an appropriately
modified definition of billiard reflection, which gives rise to Minkowski billiards, intro-
duced by E. Gutkin and S. Tabachnikov [18]. The arguments of [9, 8] can be adapted to
this new setting, implying that in any convex billiard table K the shortest closed (gener-
alized) Q-Minkowski billiard orbit exists and is 2- or 3-periodic. We denote by c(K × Q)
its length. The following result appears in [2],2 building on the ideas from [9, 8].
Theorem 2.3. A convex shape K is a translation cover for any closed curve of unit
Q-length if and only if c(K × Q) ≥ 1.
   We remark that even though we restrict our attention here to planar covers and billiards,
the papers [8, 2] work in higher dimensions; the relevant higher-dimensional notions are
straightforward generalizations of two-dimensional ones.
   The definition of c(K ×Q) is invariant with respect to translations of K, and in Section 3
we explain why one does not need to assume that the origin lies in the interior of Q, by
defining Q-lengths in a way that is translation-invariant with respect to Q as well. We
also note that if K or Q is rescaled by a positive factor, then c(K × Q) gets multiplied
by the same factor.
   The notation c(K × Q), yet to be explained, might suggest that the roles of K and Q
are somewhat symmetric. This is indeed the case; we outline the intuition very briefly.

cone of Q at the point p ∈ ∂Q, which is the momentum of q → q ′ (rescaling the velocity, we
can assume that the momentum is unit). At the point q ′ , a billiard reflection occurs, and

2.3. Symplectic systoles. We explain now how Minkowski billiards fit in the framework
of much more general symplectic dynamics. Again, for simplicity, we limit the discussion
to the lowest-dimensional scenario, although all the notions can be considered in higher
dimensions as well.
   In this subsection, it is convenient to regard R2 × R2 as a phase space with coordinates
(q1 , q2 , p1 , p2 ). The billiard table K lives in the q-space (space of positions), while the
dual unit ball Q of the norm lives in the p-space (space of momenta). The splitting of
R2 × R2 into the space of positions and the space of momenta is called lagrangian, and so
is the product K × Q. We will see how the billiard dynamics in the configuration (K, Q)
corresponds to the symplectic dynamics on the boundary of their product, ∂(K × Q) ⊂
R2 × R 2 .
   More generally, let X ⊂ R2 × R2 be any convex body, the prototype example being
X = K × Q. Its boundary ∂X is a convex hypersurface in R2 × R2 . If we pretend for
a second that ∂X is smooth, then we have two natural—and in fact equivalent—ways of
defining smooth dynamics on it.
     (1) Contact viewpoint. Consider the standard symplectic form ω = q1 ∧ p1 + q2 ∧ p2 .
           The restriction ω|∂X has a one-dimensional kernel, which can be oriented according
           to the sign of the Liouville form λ = p1 dq1 +p2 dq2 , which endows ∂X with a contact
           structure. The integral curves of ker ω|∂X are called characteristics, or Reeb flow
           trajectories, and the integral of λ along a Reeb flow trajectory is called its action.
     (2) Hamiltonian viewpoint. View ∂X as a non-degenerate level set of a smooth hamil-
           tonian H : R2 × R2 → R. The associated hamiltonian vector field X is determined
           by ω(X, ·) = −dH(·). This canonically defines characteristics as the trajectories
           of the corresponding hamiltonian flow.

   The second approach is easier to extend to the convex non-smooth case, if we re-
formulate it as follows. It is convenient to introduce the “imaginary unit” operator
J (q1 , q2 , p1 , p2 ) = (−p1 , −p2 , q1 , q2 ), so that the standard inner product on R2 × R2 is
given by ⟨x, y⟩ = ω(x, J y). Then characteristics can be equivalently defined as solutions
of the classicalRHamilton equation of motion γ̇(t) = −J ∇H(γ(t)). The action can be
rewritten as 12 ⟨γ(t), J γ̇(t)⟩dt. In the non-smooth case, the gradient direction ∇H is
replaced by the outward normal cone of K × Q, and the Hamilton differential equation
becomes a differential inclusion. It turns out that the resulting definition of Reeb dynam-
ics in ∂(K × Q) exactly matches the definition of Minkowski billiard dynamics [5], and the
action of Reeb flow trajectories in ∂(K × Q) coincides with the Q-length of corresponding
billiard trajectory in K.
   Having defined the Reeb flow, a natural question is whether periodic Reeb flow trajec-
tories exist. They are called Reeb orbits. In the setting as above, for a smooth convex
hypersurface, the existence was proven in [32, 39]. The extension to the non-smooth case
is explained in [5, Appendix]. Reeb orbits of smallest action are often called symplectic
systoles. Finally we are ready to define the mysterious quantity c(·), one of the many
symplectic invariants known as symplectic capacities.
Definition 2.4. Given a convex body X ⊂ R2 ×R2 , its Ekeland–Hofer–Zehnder symplectic
capacity c(X) is the smallest action of a Reeb orbit in ∂X.
   Without going into details, we just mention that a vibrant field of research is dedicated
to various symplectic capacities, starting from the celebrated Gromov non-squeezing the-
orem; see, e.g, [13]. Conjecture 1.1 was stated in [37] more generally, for any symplectic
capacity of any convex body, and not just the EHZ capacity of lagrangian products. A ma-
jor source of motivation for studying Conjecture 1.1 comes from the fact that it implies [3]
Mahler’s conjecture from 1939 [26], which bounds the volume product vol K · vol K ◦ of
any convex origin-symmetric body K ⊂ Rn from below by that of a cube. The proof of
the implication was significantly simplified in [2], where it was reduced to a worm prob-
lem: if K is origin-symmetric, then every closed curve of K ◦ -length 4 can be covered by
a translate of K.3
   There are other active directions of symplectic research concerned with the equality
cases in the Viterbo inequality. It happens that many of them exhibit various properties
of symplectic balls in different sense. One way a convex body X may resemble a symplectic
ball is when the interior of X is symplectomorphic to the interior of a round ball in R2 ×R2 ;
that is, when there is a diffeomorphism between them preserving the restriction of ω. This
is a strong property, and it was recently shown in [28] that it extends to a certain analogy
between the boundary ∂X and the standard round sphere S 3 . The boundary ∂X can
be similar to the sphere S 3 in terms of its Reeb flow. The Reeb flow on the standard
round sphere is exceptionally rigid: the entire S 3 decomposes into an S 2 -worth of Reeb
orbits of equal action (forming the classical Hopf fibration S 3 → S 2 ). When ∂X enjoys
similar properties to some extent, one speaks of variants of the Zoll property, which is
straightforward to define when ∂X is smooth, but becomes delicate in the non-smooth
setting. If ∂X is smooth, then the classical Zoll property forces the Viterbo inequality
to become an equality [38]. If ∂X is non-smooth, as in our case of interest X = K × Q,
a strong variant of the Zoll property was proposed in [28]; it is not known whether it
implies that the Viterbo inequality is attained with equality. We will briefly touch on
these phenomena when discussing specific equality cases. Some of them are known to be

                   3. A pivot: reduction to normal triangles
   In this section we add to the list of equivalences from Theorem 2.5, by modifying its
second item in the case when Q is a polygon. The set of “worms” will be narrowed down
from all triangles of unit Q-perimeter to a finite collection of such (possibly degenerate)
triangles whose sides are orthogonal to those of Q. First, we carefully define the Q-normed
geometry; then we introduce these distinguished triangles (Definition 3.2); and finally we
prove Theorem 3.3, which allows us to restate the Viterbo inequality as a worm problem
for a finite collection of distinguished triangles (Problem 3.4). The material of this section
can be generalized to higher dimensions in a straightforward way.

property of being Q-normal is preserved under translations, so we will oftentimes speak
about translation classes of Q-normal triangles.
3.2. Reduction to triangle covers. For two sets A, B ⊂ R2 , let us say that A fits into
B if A + t ⊂ B for some translation vector t ∈ R2 . If γ : I → R2 is a curve, and the
image of γ in R2 fits into B, then we will simply say that the curve γ fits into B. The
main result of this section is as follows.
Theorem 3.3. If every Q-normal triangle fits into K, then every closed curve of unit
Q-length fits into K. Consequently, every Q-normal triangle fits into K if and only if
c(K × Q) ≥ 1.

the following covering problem associated with Q.

is a Q-cover if every Q-normal triangle fits into K. What is the smallest Q-cover, in
terms of area? In other words: given a collection of Q-normal triangles, one from each
translation class, we would like to translate each of them in order to minimize the area of

the convex hull. Is it true that this area is at least 2 area(Q) ?

each triple of outward normal directions of Q enclosing the origin, then every Q-normal
triangle fits into K trivially. Therefore, we arrive at the following question, an affirmative
answer to which is equivalent to Conjecture 1.1 in the case when n = 2 and K is centrally

   An attempt to solve the same problem using the method of closed curves enclosing
constant area leads to the following observations. First of all, such a curve exists: if we
label the vertices of the Q-normal triangles along ∂K0 as p1 , . . . , p12 , and denote by vi
the sliding copy of pi , then Γ = (v1 → . . . → v12 → v1 ) is a curve enclosing constant

complete: why must A(Γ) ≤ area conv Γ hold?
   However, the characterization of equality cases is easier done with this method rather
than by analyzing the proof we gave. Recall that the smallest area of a Q-cover is attained
when, roughly speaking, Γ follows the boundary of ∂K counterclockwise, except for pos-
sible portions where Γ traces an arc inside K back and forth without any contribution
to the enclosed area. In Figure 13 one sees an example where this cancellation happens
thanks to the fact that p1 = p11 , p3 = p5 , and p7 = p9 , and the exact position of the yellow
triangle does not matter. Shifting the orange triangle does not change the enclosed area,
so we obtain a two-dimensional family of equality cases. They are mostly hexagons (in
Figure 13 it is the most symmetric hexagon in the family), but the boundary cases are
pentagons and quadrilaterals. The centrally symmetric image of this family yields another
two-dimensional family of equality cases. To the best of our knowledge, all these equality
cases were previously unknown. Together with K0 and −K0 , these two families exhaust
all equality cases, as can be verified by analyzing Γ.
   It is an open question whether each of these sets K gives rise to a symplectic ball K ×Q.

7.2. Non-regular hexagons. The methods of this paper fail to provably find the small-
est Q-cover for any other hexagon Q other than the affinely regular one. We conclude
by the following observation, obtained by swapping the role of K and Q in some of the
equality cases that we already found earlier, and where K turned out to be a centrally
symmetric hexagon. There is a full-dimensional open set in the space of centrally symmet-
ric hexagons such that, for any Q from this set, there are at least four shapes K attaining
equality in the Viterbo inequality. They appear to be isolated, and conjecturally, they
have the smallest area among Q-covers. See Figure 14 for one such example: K0 , K1 ,
−K0 , and −K1 are conjecturally the only area-minimizing Q-covers there. Observe that
the orange triangle slides freely inside K1 .
